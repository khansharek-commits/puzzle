## Economics

_How people, businesses and countries make choices when they can't have everything._

### Thinking Like an Economist

The five ideas every other part of economics is built on: scarcity, opportunity cost, thinking at the margin, incentives and trade.

#### Lesson 1: Scarcity: You Can't Have Everything

**Goal:** Understand why every choice involves giving something up.

**Q:** A billionaire has more money than they could ever spend. Do they face scarcity?

- ▫️ No. Scarcity only affects people without enough money.
- ✅ Yes. They still have only 24 hours a day and one life to spend them in.

*Why:* Even with unlimited money, time is limited. Choosing to spend an afternoon in Paris means not spending it in Tokyo. That is scarcity.

> **What scarcity means**
>
> Scarcity means our wants are bigger than the resources available to satisfy them. Resources include money, but also time, land, raw materials, energy and people’s skills.
>
> Scarcity isn’t the same as poverty. A rich country and a poor country both face it; they just face different trade-offs.

> **Why it matters**
>
> Because resources are scarce, every use of a resource is also a decision not to use it for something else. An hour spent studying is an hour not spent sleeping. A field planted with wheat can’t also grow corn.
>
> Economics is the study of how people make these choices, and what happens when millions of choices add up.

**Q:** A town has one empty plot of land. Which statement is the economist’s way of describing the situation?

- ▫️ The town should build whatever is most popular.
- ✅ Using the plot for a park means not using it for housing, a school or shops.
- ▫️ Land isn’t scarce because the town could buy more.

*Why:* The core economic insight is the trade-off: each possible use of the plot rules out the others. Deciding which is best comes later; first, see what is being given up.

**Q:** Which of these is NOT a scarce resource for a student preparing for exams?

- ▫️ Hours before the exam
- ▫️ Energy and focus
- ✅ The fact that 2 + 2 = 4
- ▫️ Money for tutoring

*Why:* Knowledge like 2 + 2 = 4 isn’t used up when someone uses it, so it isn’t scarce in the economic sense. Time, energy and money all run out.

**Recap**

- Scarcity: wants are greater than the resources available.
- Time, land, materials and skills are resources too, not just money.
- Every use of a resource is a choice not to use it for something else.

#### Lesson 2: Opportunity Cost: The Real Price of Anything

**Goal:** Work out what a choice really costs, including what you give up.

**Q:** A concert ticket costs 50. To go, you’d have to skip a work shift that pays 80. You would have worked otherwise. What does going to the concert really cost you?

- Answer: **130**

*Why:* You pay 50 for the ticket and give up 80 in wages. The full cost is 130. If the concert isn’t worth 130 to you, the economist says don’t go.

> **Opportunity cost**
>
> The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up.
>
> It includes money you spend, but also money you could have earned, time and anything else you sacrifice. The price on the label is often only part of the real cost.

> **Only the next-best option counts**
>
> You can only do one thing with a given hour or pound, so the opportunity cost is the value of the single best option you gave up, not the sum of all of them.
>
> If instead of studying tonight you could have worked (worth 40) or watched a film (worth 15 of enjoyment to you), the opportunity cost of studying is 40, not 55.

**Q:** You have a free Saturday. You could tutor (earning 60), help at a friend’s café (earning 45) or rest. You choose to go hiking. What is the opportunity cost of hiking, in money terms?

- Answer: **60**

*Why:* The opportunity cost is the best alternative given up: tutoring at 60. You couldn’t have done all three, so you don’t add them up.

**Q:** Going to university costs 9,000 a year in fees. A student could otherwise earn 20,000 a year working. An economist would say the yearly cost is about…

- ▫️ 9,000
- ▫️ 20,000
- ✅ 29,000
- ▫️ 11,000

*Why:* Fees (9,000) plus the earnings given up (20,000). Lost earnings are often the biggest cost of studying, which is why university is a bigger investment than the fees alone suggest.

**Q:** A city spends 10 million on a new stadium. What is the opportunity cost?

- ▫️ Nothing, if the stadium makes a profit
- ▫️ The 10 million itself
- ✅ The best other use of that 10 million, such as a hospital wing or lower taxes

*Why:* Opportunity cost is measured in what you could have had instead. When governments spend, ask: what else could this money have done?

**Recap**

- Opportunity cost = the value of the best alternative you give up.
- It includes lost time and lost earnings, not just money spent.
- Count only the single next-best option, not every option.

#### Lesson 3: Sunk Costs and Thinking at the Margin

**Goal:** Make decisions by looking forward, comparing the extra benefit with the extra cost.

**Q:** You paid 15 for a film. After 30 minutes you’re bored and would rather go for a walk. What should you do?

- ▫️ Stay, or the 15 is wasted
- ✅ Leave, if you’d enjoy the walk more than the rest of the film

*Why:* The 15 is gone whether you stay or leave. Staying doesn’t get it back; it only costs you more time.

> **Sunk costs**
>
> A sunk cost is money, time or effort already spent that you can’t get back.
>
> Because nothing you decide now can change it, a sunk cost shouldn’t affect your decision. Yet people often keep going with bad projects “because we’ve already invested so much”. This is called the sunk cost fallacy, or the Concorde fallacy, after the British and French governments that kept funding the supersonic Concorde airliner even as it became clear it would never pay back its costs.

> **Thinking at the margin**
>
> Economists call a small, extra step a marginal change: one more hour, one more worker, one more slice.
>
> The rule for good decisions is: do one more unit if its marginal benefit is greater than its marginal cost. Ignore what you have already spent; compare only what the next step adds and what it costs.

**Q:** A company has spent 2 million developing an app. Finishing it will cost another 1 million, and it’s expected to earn 600,000 in total. What should it do?

- ▫️ Finish it, so the 2 million isn’t wasted
- ✅ Stop, because finishing costs 1 million to earn 600,000
- ▫️ Finish it, because 2.6 million is more than 2 million

*Why:* The 2 million is sunk. Looking forward, finishing costs 1 million and returns 600,000: a further loss of 400,000. Stopping is better.

**Q:** A bakery sells extra cakes at 4 each. Each extra cake costs 3 in ingredients and labour. A 101st cake would cost 3.50 to make, and a 102nd would cost 4.50. How many should it make?

- ▫️ 100
- ✅ 101
- ▫️ 102

*Why:* The 101st cake adds 4 of revenue for 3.50 of cost, so make it. The 102nd adds 4 for 4.50: a loss. Stop at 101.

**Recap**

- Sunk costs are already spent and can’t be recovered, so ignore them.
- Marginal means one more unit or step.
- Do one more if its marginal benefit is greater than its marginal cost.

#### Lesson 4: Incentives: People Respond to Rewards

**Goal:** Predict how rules and prices change behaviour, including in ways nobody intended.

**Q:** In 1902, officials in Hanoi (then under French rule) paid people a reward for every rat tail they handed in, to fight a plague of rats. What do you think happened?

- ▫️ The rat population fell sharply
- ✅ People cut off tails and released the rats to breed more
- ▫️ Nobody took part

*Why:* Officials soon saw tailless rats running around the city. Some people even began breeding rats to collect more bounties. The reward was for tails, not for fewer rats.

> **What an incentive is**
>
> An incentive is anything that rewards or penalises a choice: a price, a wage, a fine, a tax, a grade, praise.
>
> When incentives change, behaviour changes. Raise the price of something and people buy less. Pay a bonus for something and people do more of it. This is one of the most reliable patterns in economics.

> **Unintended consequences**
>
> People respond to what is actually rewarded, not to what the rule-maker hoped for. The Hanoi bounty rewarded tails, so people produced tails.
>
> When you design or judge a policy, ask: what exactly does this reward, and what is the easiest way to get that reward?

**Q:** A call centre pays staff a bonus for every call they finish in under two minutes. What is the most likely unintended effect?

- ▫️ Customers get more help
- ✅ Staff rush or cut off calls, leaving problems unsolved
- ▫️ Fewer calls come in

*Why:* The bonus rewards short calls, not solved problems. Expect short calls, even if customers have to call back.

**Q:** A government adds a tax to sugary drinks. Which outcome does the incentive predict?

- ▫️ People buy more sugary drinks
- ✅ People buy fewer sugary drinks and switch to other drinks
- ▫️ Nothing changes, because people like their drinks

*Why:* A higher price is a stronger penalty for that choice, so people buy less of it. The UK’s 2018 soft drinks levy went further: many companies cut the sugar in their drinks to avoid the tax.

**Recap**

- An incentive is a reward or penalty that influences choices.
- People respond to what is actually rewarded.
- Good policy asks: what is the easiest way to get this reward?

#### Lesson 5: Trade: Why Both Sides Win

**Goal:** Use comparative advantage to see why trade helps even when one side is better at everything.

**Q:** Ana can bake bread and sew shirts faster than Ben. Can they both gain by trading?

- ▫️ No. Ana should just do everything herself.
- ✅ Yes. Each can specialise in what they give up least to make.

*Why:* Surprisingly, yes. This is comparative advantage, one of the most important and least intuitive ideas in economics.

> **Absolute and comparative advantage**
>
> In one hour, Ana can make 6 loaves or 3 shirts. Ben can make 2 loaves or 2 shirts.
>
> Ana has an absolute advantage in both: she makes more of each. But look at opportunity cost. For Ana, 1 shirt costs 2 loaves she could have baked. For Ben, 1 shirt costs only 1 loaf.
>
> Ben gives up less to make a shirt, so he has the comparative advantage in shirts. Ana has it in bread.

**Q:** For Ana, how many loaves does making 1 shirt cost her? (She can make 6 loaves or 3 shirts in an hour.)

- Answer: **2**

*Why:* 6 loaves ÷ 3 shirts = 2 loaves per shirt.

> **Why specialising helps**
>
> If Ana bakes and Ben sews, then they trade at a price between their two costs, say 1.5 loaves per shirt, both come out ahead. Ana gets shirts for 1.5 loaves instead of the 2 it would cost her to make one. Ben gets 1.5 loaves for each shirt that cost him only 1 loaf to make.
>
> The economist David Ricardo set out this idea in 1817, using England, Portugal, cloth and wine. It explains why countries, firms and people specialise and trade.

**Q:** A top surgeon types faster than her assistant. Why might she still hire the assistant to type her reports?

- ▫️ She is lazy
- ✅ Each hour she spends typing costs her an hour of much more valuable surgery
- ▫️ Typing is unimportant

*Why:* Her opportunity cost of typing is huge, so even though she is faster, the assistant has the comparative advantage in typing.

**Q:** Country A gives up 4 tonnes of wheat to make 1 car. Country B gives up 10 tonnes of wheat to make 1 car. Which country should specialise in cars?

- ✅ Country A
- ▫️ Country B
- ▫️ Neither: they should each make both

*Why:* Country A gives up less wheat per car, so it has the comparative advantage in cars. Country B should focus on wheat.

**Recap**

- Absolute advantage: you can make more of something.
- Comparative advantage: you give up less (lower opportunity cost) to make it.
- Specialising by comparative advantage and trading lets both sides gain.

### How Civilisation Began

From hunter-gatherers to farming, the first cities, writing and the first written laws.

#### Lesson 1: The Long Prehistory

**Goal:** See how recent civilisation is compared with the whole human story.

**Q:** Our species, Homo sapiens, has existed for about 300,000 years. For roughly what share of that time have humans lived in cities?

- ▫️ About half
- ▫️ About a quarter
- ✅ Less than 2%

*Why:* The first cities appeared around 6,000 years ago. That’s about 2% of our species’ history, and most people only began living in cities very recently.

> **Our species**
>
> The oldest known fossils of Homo sapiens, found at Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, are about 300,000 years old. From Africa our ancestors spread across the world, reaching Australia by at least 50,000 years ago and the Americas by around 15,000 or more years ago.
>
> For almost all of that time, everyone lived as hunter-gatherers.

> **Hunter-gatherer life**
>
> Hunter-gatherers lived in small, mobile bands, often of a few dozen people. They knew hundreds of plants and animals, moved with the seasons, and owned little they couldn’t carry.
>
> They weren’t primitive: their brains were the same as ours. They made art (cave paintings in Indonesia and Europe are over 40,000 years old), tools, clothing, boats and music.

**Q:** Compared with you, how intelligent were humans living 40,000 years ago?

- ▫️ Much less intelligent
- ✅ Biologically just as capable; they lacked accumulated knowledge, not brainpower
- ▫️ More intelligent

*Why:* Their brains were essentially modern. What changed over history was the knowledge, technology and institutions passed down and built upon.

**Q:** Put these in order, earliest first.

- Items: The first cities; Farming begins; The oldest known Homo sapiens fossils; Humans reach Australia
- Correct order: The oldest known Homo sapiens fossils → Humans reach Australia → Farming begins → The first cities

*Why:* About 300,000 years ago, at least 50,000 years ago, about 12,000 years ago, and about 6,000 years ago.

**Recap**

- Homo sapiens is about 300,000 years old.
- For about 98% of that time, humans were hunter-gatherers.
- Ancient humans had brains like ours; what grew was shared knowledge.

#### Lesson 2: The Farming Revolution

**Goal:** Understand when and where farming began, and what it changed, good and bad.

> **When and where**
>
> Around 12,000 years ago, people in the Fertile Crescent (today’s Iraq, Syria, Türkiye, Israel and nearby) began farming wheat and barley and herding sheep and goats.
>
> Farming was then invented independently in at least six or seven other places, including China (rice and millet), Mesoamerica (maize), the Andes (potatoes), New Guinea and parts of Africa. Different peoples, far apart, made the same leap.

**Q:** What is the most important thing farming made possible?

- ▫️ Less work for everyone
- ✅ A food surplus, so some people could do jobs other than finding food
- ▫️ Better health for everyone

*Why:* A surplus let populations grow and supported people who weren’t producing food: priests, craftspeople, soldiers and rulers.

> **Not all good news**
>
> Farming fed more people, but skeletons from early farming villages are often shorter and show more signs of disease and poor nutrition than those of hunter-gatherers. Farmers worked long hours, relied on a few crops that could fail, and lived crowded together with animals, which helped diseases spread.
>
> So why farm? Because farming fed far more people per square kilometre. Once populations grew, there was no going back.

**Q:** Early farmers were often less healthy than hunter-gatherers. Why did farming still spread?

- ▫️ Farmers were forced by kings from the start
- ✅ Farming supported many more people on the same land, so farming populations grew and outnumbered others
- ▫️ Hunter-gatherers asked to learn it

*Why:* More food per acre meant more people. Larger farming populations spread and outnumbered foraging groups.

> **A surprising temple**
>
> At Göbekli Tepe in Türkiye, people built huge carved stone pillars, some over 5 metres tall, around 11,500 years ago, before or right at the start of farming there. Some archaeologists think big gatherings at sites like this may have helped push people toward settling down, not the other way round.

**Q:** What does Göbekli Tepe suggest?

- ▫️ Monuments were only built after farming
- ✅ People who may still have been hunter-gatherers could organise huge building projects
- ▫️ It was built by a later empire

*Why:* It challenges the old idea that complex society always came after farming.

**Recap**

- Farming began about 12,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent, and independently elsewhere.
- The surplus let populations grow and supported non-farming jobs.
- Early farmers were often less healthy, but farming fed far more people.

#### Lesson 3: The First Cities

**Goal:** See what made cities new, and what they needed to work.

> **Uruk**
>
> One of the first true cities was Uruk, in Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in today’s Iraq. By around 3000 BCE it may have held 40,000 people or more, behind walls about 9 km long.
>
> That is nothing like a village. Most people in Uruk didn’t grow their own food.

**Q:** Tens of thousands of people live together and most don’t farm. What problem must the city solve?

- ✅ How to collect and share out food from the countryside
- ▫️ How to stop people farming
- ▫️ How to build taller walls

*Why:* Cities depend on moving food from farms to non-farmers. That needs storage, record-keeping and someone in charge.

> **What cities created**
>
> To feed and organise so many people, early cities developed:
>
> • Specialised jobs: potters, metalworkers, scribes, priests, soldiers.
> • Big temples that stored grain and handed out rations.
> • Rulers and officials to make decisions and settle disputes.
> • Social classes: some people became much richer and more powerful than others.
>
> Historians often call this package “civilisation”.

**Q:** Why were the first cities usually built beside great rivers, like the Tigris, Euphrates, Nile and Indus?

- ✅ Rivers provided water for irrigation, fertile soil and easy transport
- ▫️ Rivers kept enemies away completely
- ▫️ People liked the view

*Why:* River valleys could feed big populations, and boats moved goods far more easily than carts.

**Q:** Put the chain of causes in order.

- Items: Cities with specialists and rulers; Food surplus; Farming
- Correct order: Farming → Food surplus → Cities with specialists and rulers

*Why:* Farming made a surplus possible; the surplus supported large populations of non-farmers living in cities.

**Recap**

- Uruk in Mesopotamia was one of the first cities, perhaps 40,000+ people by 3000 BCE.
- Early cities grew along rivers that supported farming and transport.
- Cities brought specialised jobs, temples, rulers and inequality.

#### Lesson 4: The Invention of Writing

**Goal:** Learn why writing was invented and how it changed what societies could do.

**Q:** The earliest writing in Mesopotamia, from around 3200 BCE, was mostly used for what?

- ▫️ Poetry and stories
- ▫️ Religious prayers
- ✅ Accounts: recording grain, beer, livestock and workers

*Why:* Most of the earliest clay tablets are bookkeeping. Writing began as a tool for managing a city’s economy.

> **Cuneiform**
>
> The Sumerians of Mesopotamia pressed marks into wet clay with a cut reed, making wedge-shaped signs. This script is called cuneiform, from the Latin for “wedge”.
>
> At first the signs were pictures and number marks. Over centuries they came to stand for sounds and words, so people could write down anything: laws, letters, medicine and literature such as the Epic of Gilgamesh.

> **Invented more than once**
>
> Writing was invented independently at least three or four times: in Mesopotamia (about 3200 BCE), possibly in Egypt at about the same time, in China (by about 1200 BCE, on “oracle bones”), and in Mesoamerica (by the first millennium BCE).
>
> Each time it appeared in a complex society with a lot to keep track of.

**Q:** What could a society do with writing that it couldn’t easily do before?

- ✅ Remember far more than any one person, across generations and distances
- ▫️ Grow food faster
- ▫️ Stop all wars

*Why:* Writing stores information outside human memory: debts, laws, knowledge and history. It is also why the period with written records is called “history”.

**Q:** Why is the time before writing called “prehistory”?

- ▫️ Nothing important happened
- ✅ There are no written records from it, so we know it only from archaeology
- ▫️ It was before humans existed

*Why:* Prehistory is studied through bones, tools and ruins rather than texts.

**Recap**

- Writing began in Mesopotamia around 3200 BCE, as record-keeping.
- Cuneiform means “wedge-shaped”, pressed into clay.
- Writing was invented independently several times, and lets knowledge outlast memory.

#### Lesson 5: Kings and the First Written Laws

**Goal:** See how early states used written law, and whose interests it protected.

> **Hammurabi’s Code**
>
> Around 1754 BCE, Hammurabi, king of Babylon, had 282 laws carved onto a tall black stone pillar and displayed publicly. It is one of the best-preserved early law codes; the pillar is now in the Louvre in Paris.
>
> The laws covered trade, wages, debts, marriage, property, theft and injury, and set out the punishments.

**Q:** Why carve laws in stone in a public place, rather than let judges decide each case however they liked?

- ✅ So everyone could know the rules in advance, and the king’s authority looked permanent
- ▫️ Because there was no paper
- ▫️ To decorate the city

*Why:* Publishing laws made them predictable and public, and showed the king as the source of justice. Both ideas still matter today.

> **An eye for an eye, but not for everyone**
>
> Hammurabi’s code is famous for “an eye for an eye”. But punishments depended on social class. If a man put out the eye of another free man, his own eye would be put out. If he put out the eye of a commoner, he paid a fine of silver. If the victim was a slave, he paid half the slave’s value to the owner.
>
> Early laws gave order and predictability, but they also protected hierarchy.

**Q:** Under Hammurabi’s code, who decided the punishment for injuring someone?

- ▫️ The same punishment for everyone
- ✅ It depended on the social class of the victim
- ▫️ The victim chose freely

*Why:* Harming a high-status person was punished far more harshly. Equality before the law is a much later idea.

**Q:** Put these in order, earliest first.

- Items: Hammurabi’s law code; Farming begins in the Fertile Crescent; Writing begins in Mesopotamia; Humans reach Australia
- Correct order: Humans reach Australia → Farming begins in the Fertile Crescent → Writing begins in Mesopotamia → Hammurabi’s law code

*Why:* At least 50,000 years ago; about 10,000 BCE; about 3200 BCE; about 1754 BCE.

**Recap**

- Hammurabi of Babylon published 282 laws around 1754 BCE.
- Public written law made rules predictable and boosted the king’s authority.
- Punishments depended on social class: equality before the law came much later.

## Science

_How the world works, from atoms to galaxies._

### What Everything Is Made Of

Atoms, what's inside them, how they join together, and why matter can be solid, liquid or gas.

#### Lesson 1: Atoms: Unbelievably Small

**Goal:** Get a feel for how small atoms are, and how empty.

**Q:** Roughly how many atoms would you need to line up side by side to cross one millimetre?

- ▫️ About 1,000
- ▫️ About 100,000
- ✅ About 10 million
- ▫️ About 10 billion

*Why:* An atom is about 0.1 nanometres across: a ten-millionth of a millimetre. So about 10 million atoms fit across the width of a pencil line.

> **Everything is atoms**
>
> Every solid, liquid and gas around you, including you, is made of atoms. They are far too small to see even with an ordinary microscope.
>
> A single grain of sand holds tens of quintillions of atoms (a quintillion is a 1 followed by 18 zeros).

> **Mostly empty space**
>
> Almost all of an atom’s mass is packed into a tiny centre called the nucleus. The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller across than the atom itself.
>
> If an atom were the size of a football stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea on the centre spot. The rest is a cloud of electrons, which have very little mass.

**Q:** If an atom is mostly empty space, why can’t your hand pass through a table?

- ▫️ The atoms in the table are packed solid
- ✅ The electrons in your hand and the table push each other away with electrical forces
- ▫️ Gravity holds the table in place

*Why:* Electrons carry negative charge, and like charges repel. When you press on a table, the electron clouds of the two surfaces push back. What feels like “solid” is really electrical repulsion.

**Q:** Where is almost all of an atom’s mass?

- ▫️ Spread evenly through the atom
- ▫️ In the electron cloud
- ✅ In the nucleus

*Why:* More than 99.9% of an atom’s mass is in its nucleus.

**Recap**

- Everything is made of atoms, about 0.1 nanometres across.
- The nucleus is tiny but holds almost all the mass.
- Solids feel solid because electrons repel each other.

#### Lesson 2: Inside the Atom

**Goal:** Know the three particles in an atom, and what decides which element it is.

> **Three particles**
>
> Atoms are built from three kinds of particle:
>
> • Protons: positive charge, in the nucleus.
> • Neutrons: no charge, in the nucleus.
> • Electrons: negative charge, around the nucleus. Each is about 1,836 times lighter than a proton.
>
> In a normal atom the number of electrons equals the number of protons, so the charges cancel out.

> **Protons decide the element**
>
> The number of protons is called the atomic number, and it decides which element an atom is. Every atom with 1 proton is hydrogen. Every atom with 6 is carbon. Every atom with 79 is gold.
>
> Change the number of protons and you have a different element.

**Q:** An atom has 8 protons, 8 neutrons and 8 electrons. Another has 8 protons, 10 neutrons and 8 electrons. Are they the same element?

- ✅ Yes, both have 8 protons
- ▫️ No, they have different numbers of neutrons

*Why:* Both have 8 protons, so both are oxygen. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are called isotopes.

> **Isotopes**
>
> Isotopes are versions of an element with different numbers of neutrons. Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Carbon-14 has 6 protons and 8 neutrons.
>
> Carbon-14 is unstable and slowly decays at a known rate. Measuring how much is left in old bones or wood lets scientists date things up to about 50,000 years old: radiocarbon dating.

**Q:** Carbon-14 has 6 protons. The 14 is the total number of protons plus neutrons. How many neutrons does it have?

- Answer: **8**

*Why:* 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons.

**Q:** A neutral atom has 11 protons. How many electrons does it have?

- Answer: **11**

*Why:* In a neutral atom, electrons equal protons. This atom is sodium.

**Recap**

- Protons (+) and neutrons (0) sit in the nucleus; electrons (−) surround it.
- The number of protons (atomic number) decides the element.
- Isotopes have the same protons but different neutrons.

#### Lesson 3: The Periodic Table

**Goal:** Understand how the periodic table is organised and why it predicts how elements behave.

> **A table of all the elements**
>
> There are 118 known elements, and about 94 of them occur naturally on Earth. The periodic table lists them in order of atomic number: 1 (hydrogen), 2 (helium), 3 (lithium) and so on.
>
> Its rows wrap around so that elements with similar behaviour line up in the same column, called a group.

> **Why columns behave alike**
>
> How an element reacts depends mostly on its outermost electrons. Elements in the same column have the same number of outer electrons, so they behave in similar ways.
>
> The far-right column holds the noble gases (helium, neon, argon…). Their outer electron shells are full, so they hardly react with anything. The far-left column holds metals like sodium and potassium, which have one outer electron they give away easily, so they react violently with water.

**Q:** Why is helium, not hydrogen, used to fill party balloons and airships today?

- ▫️ Helium is lighter
- ✅ Helium is a noble gas and won’t burn; hydrogen is highly flammable
- ▫️ Hydrogen is too expensive

*Why:* Hydrogen is actually lighter, but it burns explosively. The airship Hindenburg, filled with hydrogen, caught fire in 1937. Helium’s full outer shell makes it unreactive.

> **A table that made predictions**
>
> In 1869 the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the known elements this way and left gaps where the pattern said an element should exist. He predicted the properties of those missing elements. When gallium (1875) and germanium (1886) were discovered, they matched his predictions closely.
>
> A good scientific idea doesn’t just organise what we know; it predicts what we don’t.

**Q:** Potassium is in the same column as sodium. What would you predict about potassium?

- ▫️ It is an unreactive gas
- ✅ It is a reactive metal that reacts strongly with water
- ▫️ It behaves completely differently from sodium

*Why:* Same column, same number of outer electrons, similar behaviour. Potassium reacts even more violently with water than sodium.

**Recap**

- 118 known elements, ordered by atomic number.
- Columns (groups) share outer-electron patterns, so they behave alike.
- Mendeleev’s table predicted elements before they were found.

#### Lesson 4: Molecules and Bonds

**Goal:** See how atoms join, and why compounds can be nothing like the elements that make them.

**Q:** Sodium is a soft metal that explodes in water. Chlorine is a poisonous green gas. What do you get when they combine?

- ▫️ An even more dangerous poison
- ✅ Table salt
- ▫️ A metal gas

*Why:* Sodium chloride is ordinary table salt. When elements combine into a compound, the result can have completely different properties from its ingredients.

> **Why atoms bond**
>
> Atoms are most stable with a full outer shell of electrons. They get there by bonding with other atoms:
>
> • Ionic bonds: one atom gives electrons to another. Sodium gives its single outer electron to chlorine. Now sodium is positive and chlorine is negative, and opposite charges hold them together.
> • Covalent bonds: atoms share pairs of electrons. In water (H₂O), an oxygen atom shares electrons with two hydrogen atoms.

**Q:** In a water molecule, the oxygen atom and the hydrogen atoms…

- ▫️ swap protons
- ✅ share pairs of electrons (covalent bonds)
- ▫️ are held by magnets

*Why:* Water is held together by covalent bonds: shared electron pairs.

> **Formulas**
>
> A chemical formula tells you which atoms are in a molecule and how many. H₂O means 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. CO₂ is 1 carbon and 2 oxygens. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is 6 carbons, 12 hydrogens and 6 oxygens.

**Q:** How many atoms in total are in one molecule of glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆?

- Answer: **24**

*Why:* 6 + 12 + 6 = 24 atoms.

**Recap**

- Atoms bond to reach a full outer shell of electrons.
- Ionic bonds transfer electrons; covalent bonds share them.
- A compound can behave nothing like its elements: toxic chlorine plus explosive sodium makes salt.

#### Lesson 5: Solids, Liquids and Gases

**Goal:** Explain states of matter and temperature in terms of moving particles.

> **Particles never stop moving**
>
> The atoms and molecules in everything are always moving. Temperature is a measure of how fast, on average: the hotter something is, the faster its particles move.
>
> • In a solid, particles vibrate in fixed positions.
> • In a liquid, they slide past each other.
> • In a gas, they fly around freely, far apart.

**Q:** What happens to water molecules as you heat ice until it melts and then boils?

- ▫️ They get bigger
- ✅ They move faster, until they break free of each other
- ▫️ They turn into different atoms

*Why:* The molecules stay the same size and stay H₂O. Heating makes them move faster until they overcome the forces holding them together.

> **As cold as it gets**
>
> If you could slow particles down until their movement reached its minimum, you would reach absolute zero: −273.15 °C. Nothing can be colder.
>
> Scientists use the Kelvin scale, which starts at absolute zero. 0 K is −273.15 °C, and water freezes at about 273 K.

**Q:** Most substances shrink when they freeze. Ice does the opposite and floats on water. Why does that matter for life?

- ▫️ It doesn’t matter
- ✅ Lakes freeze from the top down, so the ice insulates the water beneath and fish survive winter
- ▫️ It makes ice melt faster

*Why:* Ice is about 9% less dense than liquid water, because its molecules lock into an open crystal pattern. If ice sank, lakes could freeze solid from the bottom up.

**Q:** Room temperature is about 20 °C. Roughly what is that in kelvin? (Add 273.)

- Answer: **293**

*Why:* 20 + 273 = 293 K.

**Recap**

- Temperature measures how fast particles move on average.
- Solid: vibrating in place. Liquid: sliding. Gas: flying freely.
- Absolute zero is −273.15 °C (0 K). Ice floats because it is less dense than water.
